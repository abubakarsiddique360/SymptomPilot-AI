## Overview

Anaphylaxis is a severe, rapidly developing allergic reaction that can affect
breathing and blood pressure. Common triggers include foods such as nuts, shellfish
and eggs, insect stings, latex, and some medicines. Symptoms usually begin within
minutes of exposure but can start up to a few hours later.

## Warning signs

Call emergency services immediately if any of these appear:

- Swelling of the tongue, lips, throat or face.
- Difficulty breathing, wheezing, a hoarse voice, or a feeling the throat is closing.
- Widespread hives with vomiting, dizziness or breathlessness.
- A sudden drop in blood pressure causing faintness, collapse or confusion.
- A sense of impending doom with any of the above.

## What to do

Use a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector without delay, injecting into the outer
thigh as the device instructions describe, and call emergency services. Lay the
person flat with legs raised unless breathing is easier sitting up; do not let
them stand or walk suddenly. A second dose from a spare device can be given after
five minutes if there is no improvement and the dispatcher agrees. Antihistamine
tablets do not treat anaphylaxis and must never delay adrenaline.

## After the reaction

Anyone treated for anaphylaxis needs hospital observation, because symptoms can
return hours later without further exposure. Ask for a written allergy plan, a
replacement auto-injector, and referral to an allergy specialist. Document what
was eaten, touched or injected, and keep the packaging if possible.

## Preventing recurrence

Read food labels carefully, tell restaurants about the allergy, carry two devices
if prescribed, and check expiry dates. Teach family, friends and colleagues how to
use the device and when to call for help.

## Limits of this guidance

This page cannot confirm whether a reaction is allergic or whether it will
progress. If breathing, swallowing or circulation is affected, treat it as an
emergency and call for help immediately.
