## Overview

Chest pain has many causes. Most of them are not a heart attack, but a heart
attack is the cause that must be excluded first because treatment works best in
the first hours. Pain from the heart is often described as pressure, heaviness,
squeezing or tightness rather than a sharp stab, and it may spread to the arm,
jaw, neck, back or stomach.

## Warning signs of a possible heart attack

- Pressure or tightness in the centre of the chest lasting more than a few minutes.
- Pain spreading to the left arm, both arms, jaw, neck or upper abdomen.
- Sweating, nausea, breathlessness, or a feeling of doom.
- Pain that comes on with exertion and eases with rest, then starts happening at rest.
- Chest discomfort with fainting, or with a pulse that feels very fast or irregular.

Women, older adults, and people with diabetes sometimes notice breathlessness,
extreme tiredness, indigestion-like discomfort or nausea instead of classic chest
pain. That pattern still needs urgent assessment.

## What to do now

Call emergency services and stop physical activity. Sit down, loosen tight
clothing, and stay with the person. If the person is awake and not allergic, the
emergency dispatcher may advise chewing aspirin; follow their instruction rather
than acting on your own. Do not drive yourself to hospital with active chest
pain, because the risk of collapse during the journey is real.

If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, start
cardiopulmonary resuscitation if you are trained and ask for a defibrillator.

## Other causes

Chest discomfort can also come from the chest wall, acid reflux, anxiety, a
collapsed lung, inflammation of the heart lining, or a blood clot in the lung.
These explanations are never assumed before a heart cause has been considered,
and a normal resting test does not exclude a cardiac cause on its own.

## When to seek care

Any new chest pain that lasts, recurs, or comes with the warning signs above
needs emergency assessment. Pain that is clearly related to a recent strain, is
reproduced by pressing on the chest wall, and settles with rest may still deserve
a routine appointment, especially if you have risk factors such as smoking,
diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history of early heart disease.

## Limits of this guidance

This page cannot tell you whether your chest pain is cardiac. Only a clinician
with an examination and tests such as an electrocardiogram can do that.
