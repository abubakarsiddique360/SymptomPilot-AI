## Overview

Fever is a raised body temperature, usually defined as 38 °C (100.4 °F) or above
when measured with a reliable thermometer. It is a normal immune response to
infection and is not harmful by itself. What matters is the age of the person, how
they look and behave, how long the fever lasts, and which other symptoms come
with it.

## Warning signs

Call emergency services if a fever is accompanied by any of the following:

- A baby younger than three months with a temperature of 38 °C or more.
- Stiff neck, severe headache, confusion, or a rash that does not fade when pressed.
- Difficulty breathing, blue lips, or very fast breathing.
- A seizure, unresponsiveness, or unusual drowsiness.
- Severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, or signs of dehydration.
- A fever after returning from an area where malaria is common.

Same-day assessment is appropriate for a fever above 39 °C (102.2 °F), a fever
lasting more than three days, a fever with a new rash, or fever in someone whose
immune system is weakened by illness or treatment.

## Measuring temperature

Use a digital thermometer. Oral, ear or rectal readings are more reliable than
forehead strips, which are easily affected by room temperature and sweat. Record
the reading, the time and the method, because trends over hours are more useful
than a single number.

## What may help

Rest, drink fluids regularly, and wear light clothing. A lukewarm sponge is
comfortable but does not treat the cause. Medicines that reduce fever can ease
discomfort; use only products that are appropriate for the person's age and
follow the label or a pharmacist's advice. Do not give aspirin to children or
teenagers. Never use ice baths or alcohol rubs.

## Chills and sweating

Shivering and feeling cold often occur as temperature rises, and sweating occurs
as it falls. These swings are common and are not automatically a sign of danger,
but repeated rigors with a very high fever, or fever with a fast heartbeat and
breathlessness, need urgent review.

## Limits of this guidance

Fever is a sign, not a diagnosis. This page cannot identify the cause of a fever
and does not replace examination, especially in infants, older adults, pregnant
people, and anyone receiving treatment that suppresses the immune system.
