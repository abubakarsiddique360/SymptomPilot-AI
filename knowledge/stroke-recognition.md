## Overview

A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is blocked or when a blood
vessel in the brain bursts. Brain tissue loses oxygen within minutes, and
treatment such as clot-dissolving medicine or clot retrieval is only possible in
the first hours after symptoms begin. Recognising it early is the single most
useful thing a bystander can do.

## The FAST check

- Face: ask the person to smile. Is one side drooping or uneven?
- Arms: ask them to raise both arms. Does one drift down?
- Speech: ask them to repeat a simple sentence. Is it slurred, jumbled or absent?
- Time: note the exact time symptoms started and call emergency services now.

Other signs include sudden loss of vision in one eye, sudden double vision,
sudden severe imbalance or vertigo with vomiting, sudden numbness on one side,
and sudden confusion or difficulty understanding speech.

## What to do

Call emergency services immediately, state that you suspect a stroke, and give
the time the symptoms began. Keep the person still, seated or lying on their side
if drowsy, and loosen tight clothing. Do not give food, drink or any medicine,
because swallowing may be unsafe. Do not wait to see whether symptoms improve:
transient symptoms that resolve still require emergency assessment.

## Why time matters

Every minute without treatment means more brain tissue is lost. Some strokes are
caused by a clot and can be treated with clot-dissolving medicine within a narrow
time window, while others are caused by bleeding and need different treatment.
Only imaging in hospital can tell them apart.

## Risk factors and prevention

High blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, high cholesterol, irregular heart rhythm,
obesity and physical inactivity raise stroke risk. Controlling blood pressure,
stopping smoking, and taking prescribed medicines consistently are the most
effective prevention steps.

## Limits of this guidance

You cannot confirm or exclude a stroke from symptoms alone, and neither can this
assistant. When in doubt, call emergency services; a false alarm is far safer than
a missed stroke.
